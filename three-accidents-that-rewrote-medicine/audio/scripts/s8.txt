Mass producing vitamin C required a completely different kind of chemistry, one that blends synthetic chemistry with biology. In 1933, Swiss chemist Tadeusz Reichstein, working with colleague Hans Grüssner, designed a process that could turn ordinary glucose into ascorbic acid at industrial scale. The Reichstein-Grüssner process runs in five main stages. First, glucose is chemically hydrogenated into a compound called D-sorbitol. Then a bacterium, Gluconobacter oxydans, does the next step biologically, fermenting the sorbitol into L-sorbose. From there, a series of chemical reactions, treatment with acetone, catalytic oxidation, and acid hydrolysis, convert that intermediate into the final ascorbic acid molecule. It's an elegant hybrid: half fermentation vat, half chemistry lab. Remarkably, more than ninety years after Reichstein first published the method, a version of this same process, with only minor modifications, still supplies the majority of the world's vitamin C, more than sixty thousand tons of it every year. It's energy intensive and requires careful handling of strong acids and industrial waste, but nothing has fully replaced it as the industry's backbone.
