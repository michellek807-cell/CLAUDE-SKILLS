By the 1890s, chemists understood the problem with willow bark's active ingredient well enough to try fixing it directly. At Bayer, in Germany, a young chemist named Felix Hoffmann had personal motivation: his father suffered from crippling rheumatism and couldn't tolerate the stomach destroying side effects of sodium salicylate, the standard treatment of the era. Hoffmann set out to build a gentler version of the same medicine. Chemists had actually synthesized a crude form of this compound before. Charles Frédéric Gerhardt managed it back in 1853, but his version was impure and unstable, and he abandoned the work. Hoffmann picked up where that early attempt left off. On August 10th, 1897, he successfully re-synthesized acetylsalicylic acid in a form that was finally pure, stable, and consistent. Bayer recognized what they had. On March 6th, 1899, the company trademarked the name Aspirin, A for acetyl, and spirin from Spirea, the plant genus that also produces salicylic acid. It became one of the first medicines ever marketed directly to the public, sold first as a powder, then, by 1900, as a tablet.
