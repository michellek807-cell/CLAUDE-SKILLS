Isolating a compound is one thing. Proving what it actually does is another. When Szent-Györgyi first identified hexuronic acid in 1928, he had no idea it was connected to scurvy, the disease that had plagued sailors, soldiers, and prisoners for centuries, caused by a simple lack of vitamin C in the diet. It took four more years of research, and one especially patient experiment, to make the connection. Working with colleague Joseph Svirbely at the University of Szeged, Szent-Györgyi tested the compound on guinea pigs, one of the few animals, like humans, that can't manufacture their own vitamin C internally. In April of 1932, the two scientists announced their results: a daily dose of just one milligram of hexuronic acid had protected the guinea pigs from scurvy for fifty six straight days. That was the proof. Around the same time, researchers confirmed hexuronic acid and the long sought anti scurvy vitamin were the same molecule. It was renamed accordingly, ascorbic acid, literally anti scurvy acid, and Szent-Györgyi would go on to win the Nobel Prize for the discovery.
