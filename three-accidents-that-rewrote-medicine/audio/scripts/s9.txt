Penicillin very nearly stayed a scientific footnote. In 1941, the United States didn't have enough of the drug stockpiled to treat a single patient. What changed everything was the Second World War, and a research lab in Peoria, Illinois. In 1941, Howard Florey and Norman Heatley, the British researchers who had picked up Fleming's original work, traveled to the U.S. Department of Agriculture's Northern Regional Research Laboratory looking for a way to grow penicillin at scale. The breakthrough came from an unglamorous source: corn steep liquor, a cheap byproduct of corn processing, turned out to be an ideal nutrient for growing the mold in bulk. Researchers also found a better producing strain of Penicillium growing on a moldy cantaloupe at a Peoria fruit market. Combined with a new technique, deep tank fermentation, which aerated huge vats instead of relying on shallow surface dishes, production scaled dramatically. By March of 1944, Pfizer was manufacturing penicillin in a converted Brooklyn ice factory fitted with fourteen nine thousand gallon fermentation tanks. By the time Allied forces landed at Normandy that June, there was enough penicillin on hand to treat every soldier who needed it.
