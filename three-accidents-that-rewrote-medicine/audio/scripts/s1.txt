Two thousand years before it had a name, medicine was already sitting inside a tree. Ancient Egyptian physicians ground willow bark and leaves into remedies for pain and swelling. Centuries later, Hippocrates prescribed the same bark, chewed or brewed as tea, to ease fever and childbirth pain. Inside that bark is a compound called salicin. Once it reaches the body, salicin converts into salicylic acid, the same active ingredient that still fights pain and fever today. But raw salicylic acid comes with a cost. Taken directly, it tears at the stomach lining, causing nausea, ulcers, even internal bleeding. For nearly two thousand years, that trade-off was simply the price of relief. Nobody had found a way to keep the medicine and lose the damage. That problem would sit unsolved until a nineteenth century chemist, working for his own father's sake, found a way through it.
