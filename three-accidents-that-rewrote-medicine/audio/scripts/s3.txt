In a cramped London laboratory in the late summer of 1928, a bacteriologist named Alexander Fleming was getting ready to leave for a two week vacation. Before he left, a petri dish of Staphylococcus bacteria sat forgotten on his bench, uncovered, unincubated. Somewhere in that window, a stray mold spore drifted in. Maybe through an open window. Maybe up a stairwell from another lab downstairs, where different molds were being cultured for unrelated research. Nobody planned it. Nobody controlled for it. It was simply an accident of ventilation and timing. When Fleming returned, he almost threw the ruined dish away. Instead, he looked closer. Around the patch of blue green mold, growing in his neglected culture, the bacteria were dead. Something in that mold was killing them, and Fleming had no idea yet how significant that was.
