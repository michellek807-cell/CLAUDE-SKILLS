Nearly a century ago, a Hungarian biochemist named Albert Szent-Györgyi was working with something unglamorous: ox adrenal glands, sourced from a slaughterhouse. Inside them, he found a strange, acidic, reducing compound, one that seemed to protect tissue from oxidation. He called it hexuronic acid, because he didn't yet know what it did. There was only one problem: adrenal glands are scarce, and Szent-Györgyi had nearly exhausted his supply. He needed a bigger source, fast. The answer was sitting in kitchens across Hungary. Szeged, where he worked, was the center of the country's paprika industry. On a hunch, he tested the dried red pepper, and found it was a spectacularly rich source of the same compound. Within a few years, he and his colleagues were extracting it from paprika by the kilogram, turning a kitchen spice into a laboratory reagent.
