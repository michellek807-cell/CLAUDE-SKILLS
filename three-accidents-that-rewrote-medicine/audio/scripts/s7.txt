Turning a laboratory discovery into a product every pharmacy could stock meant industrializing the chemistry behind it. Modern aspirin production starts with phenol. Treated with sodium hydroxide and carbon dioxide under heat and pressure, a reaction called the Kolbe-Schmitt reaction, phenol becomes sodium salicylate, which is then acidified into high purity salicylic acid. That's step one. Step two is the reaction that actually creates aspirin: salicylic acid is combined with acetic anhydride, using a small amount of sulfuric or phosphoric acid as a catalyst, and heated to somewhere between seventy and ninety degrees Celsius. This acetylation reaction is the heart of the whole process. Once it's complete, the mixture is cooled, allowing crude aspirin crystals to form, which are then separated out by vacuum filtration and purified through recrystallization, typically in ethanol or water. Most of the world's aspirin is still made this way, in batches, though newer plants have started experimenting with continuous production. From there, turning the purified compound into the tablet in your medicine cabinet takes just three more ingredients: corn starch, water, and a lubricant, compressed together under pressure.
