The exact date matters here: September 3rd, 1928. That's when Alexander Fleming, back from vacation, sat down and actually studied the mold growing in his abandoned petri dish. He identified it as a rare strain of Penicillium, related to the mold found on spoiled bread, but not identical to it. Around the mold colony, a clear ring had formed where the Staphylococcus bacteria simply weren't growing anymore. Something the mold was producing had killed them, or stopped them cold. Fleming named the active substance after its source: penicillin. He published his findings in 1929, in the British Journal of Experimental Pathology. And then, almost nothing happened. The paper drew little attention. Fleming himself struggled to purify enough of the compound to prove it could work as a real treatment, and he eventually moved on to other research. It would take another decade, a different team of scientists, and a different country to turn this laboratory curiosity into a medicine that could actually save lives at scale.
